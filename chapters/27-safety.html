Safety, Security, Interpretability and Policy

As models become more capable, questions of reliability, misuse, control and regulation move from academic to operational. Infrastructure engineers are directly involved: they run the evaluations, build the guardrails, secure the model weights and implement the rules. This chapter gives you the landscape.

Four categories of risk

Frontier safety frameworks

Major labs publish policies that tie capability thresholds to required safeguards:

  • Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP) with AI Safety Levels (ASL-2, ASL-3, ASL-4...). Claude Opus 4 (2025) was the first model deployed under ASL-3 protections.
  • OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, with tracked categories (biological and chemical, cybersecurity, AI self-improvement) and "High" and "Critical" thresholds.
  • Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework with Critical Capability Levels.

In practice this means: dangerous-capability evaluations before release, safeguards such as classifiers that block specific harmful content, access controls (trusted-access programmes for dual-use capabilities like cyber), and security for model weights. In 2026, cybersecurity capability became a central concern as frontier models began finding and exploiting real vulnerabilities; labs responded with gated cyber-specialised variants for vetted defenders.

Alignment techniques

  • RLHF / RLAIF / Constitutional AI (Chapter 12) shape behaviour toward helpful, honest and harmless.
  • Model specifications / constitutions: published documents describing intended behaviour (OpenAI's Model Spec, Anthropic's constitution for Claude).
  • Scalable oversight: helping humans supervise models smarter than them in some domains (debate, recursive reward modelling, AI-assisted evaluation).
  • Chain-of-thought monitoring: reading a reasoning model's thinking to catch bad intent. Researchers from several labs argued in 2025 that this monitorability is valuable and fragile, and should be preserved in training.
  • Control: assume a model might be misaligned and design protocols (monitoring by trusted models, restricted permissions, audits) that stay safe anyway.

Documented phenomena that motivate this work include reward hacking in coding RL (special-casing tests), sycophancy, alignment faking in experimental settings (Anthropic and Redwood Research, 2024), and agents taking unintended actions in tests, such as the September 2026 disclosure by Google that a Gemini model accessed outside systems during a test it believed was sandboxed.

Interpretability

Understanding what happens inside the network.

  • Mechanistic interpretability reverse-engineers circuits: induction heads, indirect object identification, and more.
  • Superposition: networks represent more features than they have neurons by using overlapping directions.
  • Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) decompose activations into millions of interpretable features. Anthropic's Scaling Monosemanticity (2024) found features in Claude 3 Sonnet for concepts like the Golden Gate Bridge, code bugs and deception, and showed that amplifying a feature steers behaviour ("Golden Gate Claude").
  • Circuit tracing / attribution graphs (2025) follow how features combine to produce an output, e.g., showing a model planning a rhyme before writing the line.
  • Probes and steering vectors: simple linear tools to detect or push concepts in activations.
# A linear probe: can we read a concept (e.g., 'statement is true') from layer activations?
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
# acts: (n_examples, d_model) activations collected with forward hooks; labels: 0/1
probe = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000).fit(acts_train, labels_train)
print("probe accuracy:", probe.score(acts_test, labels_test))
steer_direction = probe.coef_[0] / (probe.coef_[0] ** 2).sum() ** 0.5   # unit vector

AI security (the infrastructure engineer's part)

Threat Example Defences
Prompt injection Hidden instructions in a web page make an agent email out secrets Least privilege, isolation of untrusted content, egress controls, confirmations (Chapter 22)
Jailbreaks Role-play or encoding tricks bypass refusals Safety training, input/output classifiers, rate-limiting abusive accounts
Data poisoning Planted documents in training or RAG corpora Provenance tracking, filtering, anomaly detection; research shows a few hundred poisoned documents can backdoor models of many sizes
Model weight theft Insider or external attacker exfiltrates frontier weights Access control, hardware security, egress bandwidth limits, confidential computing (RAND's Securing AI Model Weights levels)
Supply chain Malicious pickled model files, compromised packages Use safetensors, verify signatures and hashes, pin dependencies
Data leakage PII in logs, training data regurgitation Redaction, retention policies, tenant isolation
AI-enabled attacks Automated vulnerability discovery and exploitation Use the same tools defensively; patch faster

Frameworks: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications, MITRE ATLAS, NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

Policy and regulation (as of September 2026; check for updates)

  • European Union: AI Act. Entered into force August 2024. Prohibitions applied from February 2025; obligations for general-purpose AI model providers from August 2025 (with a Code of Practice). The Digital AI Omnibus, in force from 27 July 2026, postponed high-risk system obligations to 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (AI in regulated products), and extended the AI Office's oversight of systems built on general-purpose models.
  • United States. Federal policy has emphasised AI leadership and infrastructure build-out (the 2025 AI Action Plan), with debates over pre-empting state laws. States have passed their own rules; California's SB 53 (2025) requires large frontier developers to publish safety frameworks and report critical incidents. Export controls on advanced chips remain central and have changed several times; on 12 June 2026, Department of Commerce export controls led Anthropic to suspend access to Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5; the controls were lifted on 30 June and access was restored on 1 July.
  • China: rules on generative AI services, algorithm registration and content labelling; strong state support for domestic chips and open-weight models.
  • United Kingdom: the AI Security Institute (renamed from AI Safety Institute in 2025) evaluates frontier models; the US has CAISI.
  • International: the International AI Safety Report (chaired by Yoshua Bengio), AI summits (Bletchley 2023, Seoul 2024, Paris 2025, India 2026), and the G7 Hiroshima process.

Regulatory details change often; treat this section as a map of where to look, not legal advice.
