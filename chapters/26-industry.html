The AI Industry Map

To have knowledge "like no one else", you need the business picture as well as the technical one: who builds what, who pays whom, where the bottlenecks and margins are, and where the jobs are. This chapter maps the industry layer by layer.

The stack as an industry

Layer by layer

Foundations and chips

  • TSMC fabricates nearly every leading AI accelerator (NVIDIA, AMD, Google TPU, AWS Trainium, Apple). Its CoWoS advanced packaging capacity has been a chronic bottleneck.
  • HBM memory makers (SK hynix leads, then Samsung and Micron) are the other choke point; HBM is a large fraction of accelerator cost.
  • NVIDIA holds the large majority of the AI accelerator market, with data-center revenue in the hundreds of billions per year by 2026 and a platform strategy spanning chips, NVLink, networking, software (CUDA, Dynamo, NeMo, NIM) and whole racks.
  • Custom silicon (ASICs): Google TPU (designed with Broadcom), AWS Trainium (with Marvell and Alchip), Meta MTIA, Microsoft Maia, and OpenAI's custom chip with Broadcom. Hyperscalers want to reduce dependence on NVIDIA's margins.
  • AMD is the main merchant-GPU alternative, with MI300/MI350 series and a 2026 rack-scale push; OpenAI signed a multi-gigawatt deal with AMD in 2025.

Cloud and compute

  • Hyperscalers: AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle (OCI grew quickly on AI deals), plus Alibaba Cloud and others in China.
  • Neoclouds: CoreWeave (public since 2025), Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe, Together AI, Fluidstack and many more. They specialise in large GPU clusters, often with contracts from labs and hyperscalers.
  • Deal structures have become financial engineering: multi-year take-or-pay contracts, GPUs used as loan collateral, vendor financing, and circular investments (chip makers investing in the labs that buy their chips). Understanding these helps you read the news critically.

Open vs closed: in 2026, the best open-weight models trail the best closed ones by a few months to a year on most benchmarks, and occasionally lead in specific areas. Open weights matter for data residency, customisation, cost at scale, and research. Licences differ (MIT and Apache 2.0 vs custom licences with usage restrictions), so read them.

Inference platforms

Together AI, Fireworks, Baseten, Groq, Cerebras, DeepInfra, Replicate, Modal, OpenRouter, AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex AI, Azure AI Foundry. They compete on price per token, latency, model selection and reliability. Margins depend on exactly the inference engineering you learned in Chapter 19.

Tooling

Vector databases, LLM gateways, evaluation and observability (Langfuse, Braintrust, Arize, LangSmith), agent frameworks, data labelling and RL-environment vendors (Scale AI, Surge AI, Mercor, Turing, Labelbox, Handshake), GPU orchestration (Run:ai, now part of NVIDIA; SkyPilot; dstack), and sandbox providers (E2B, Daytona, Modal).

Applications

  • Horizontal assistants: ChatGPT (hundreds of millions of weekly users), Claude, Gemini, Meta AI, Microsoft Copilot, Grok, Perplexity.
  • Coding: the breakout category of 2025–26. Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, Windsurf/Cognition, Replit, Lovable, Bolt, v0. Several reached hundreds of millions to billions of dollars in annualised revenue within a year or two.
  • Vertical AI: Harvey (legal), Abridge and Ambience (clinical notes), Hebbia (finance), Sierra and Decagon (customer service), Glean (enterprise search).
  • Consumer media: image, video, voice and companion apps.

Reading the industry critically

  • Benchmarks in announcements are chosen by the announcer. Look for independent evaluations (Artificial Analysis, Epoch AI, LMArena, SWE-bench leaderboards, METR).
  • "Annualised revenue run-rate" is the latest month × 12; useful for fast-growing firms, but not the same as annual revenue.
  • Capex announcements often span many years and include options, not firm spending.
  • Price per token comparisons must account for tokenizer differences, cached-input discounts, reasoning tokens and output length.
