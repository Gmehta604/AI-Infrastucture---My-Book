Numerics: BF16, FP8 and FP4

Halving the bits per number is the cheapest speed-up in AI: half the memory, half the bandwidth, and on tensor cores roughly twice the FLOP/s. Every generation of hardware has pushed lower, from FP32 to BF16 to FP8 to FP4. This deep dive explains how these formats work bit by bit, what breaks when you shrink them, and the scaling and accumulation tricks that let frontier models train in FP8 and serve in FP4.

1. Why precision is the cheapest speed-up

Every byte saved per number pays off three times:

Benefit Why
Memory capacity A 70B model needs 140 GB in BF16, 70 GB in FP8, ~37 GB in 4-bit: fewer GPUs, bigger batches, longer contexts
Memory bandwidth Memory-bound work (decode, normalisations, optimizer steps) speeds up nearly in proportion to bytes saved
Compute Tensor cores roughly double their FLOP/s each time the input width halves

H100 dense tensor-core throughput illustrates the third point: TF32 ~495 TFLOP/s, BF16 ~989, FP8 ~1,979. Blackwell adds FP4 at roughly double FP8 again, and Rubin's vendor figures lean even harder on NVFP4 (Deep Dive 15a).

The catch is that fewer bits mean less range (how large and small a number can be) and less precision (how finely numbers are spaced). The rest of this chapter is about spending bits where they matter.

2. Floating point from the bits up

A floating-point number has three fields:

value = (−1)^sign × 1.mantissa × 2^(exponent − bias) (normal numbers) value = (−1)^sign × 0.mantissa × 2^(1 − bias) (subnormals: exponent field = 0)
  • The sign bit is 0 for positive, 1 for negative.
  • The exponent field (e bits) sets the scale. It is stored with a bias of 2^(e−1) − 1 so that negative exponents can be represented: for BF16 and FP32 (8 exponent bits) the bias is 127.
  • The mantissa (or significand) field (m bits) holds the fraction after an implicit leading 1. It sets the precision: there are 2^m evenly spaced values between each pair of powers of two.
Worked example

How is 0.1 stored in BF16? Running python float_formats.py prints the bits:

FP32: 0 01111011 10011001100110011001101 = 0.1000000015 BF16: 0 01111011 1001100 = 0.1000976562

The exponent field 01111011 is 123; 123 − 127 = −4, so the scale is 2⁻⁴ = 0.0625. The BF16 mantissa 1001100 is 76/128 = 0.59375, so the value is 1.59375 × 0.0625 = 0.0996… before rounding. BF16 rounds to the nearest representable value, giving 1.6015625 × 0.0625 = 0.1000977. BF16 is literally the top 16 bits of FP32 (with rounding), which is why converting between them is cheap and why BF16 has exactly FP32's range.

Precision: machine epsilon and ULPs

Machine epsilon is the gap between 1 and the next representable number: 2⁻ᵐ. For BF16 (m = 7) it is 2⁻⁷ ≈ 0.0078, about two significant decimal digits. A ULP (unit in the last place) is the gap at any given magnitude; it doubles with each power of two. Relative precision is roughly constant across the range, which is what makes floating point suit neural networks, whose values span many orders of magnitude.

Special values

  • Zero has exponent and mantissa fields of zero (and there is a −0).
  • Subnormals (exponent field 0) fill the gap between zero and the smallest normal number, with gradually decreasing precision.
  • Infinity and NaN use the all-ones exponent in IEEE formats (FP32, FP16, BF16, E5M2).
  • FP8 E4M3 (the "fn" variant used by NVIDIA and in PyTorch's float8_e4m3fn) gives up infinity to gain range: only the bit pattern S.1111.111 is NaN, so its largest value is 1.75 × 2⁸ = 448. AMD's MI300 series used slightly different "fnuz" variants (no negative zero, different bias); newer AMD parts support the OCP standard formats.

Rounding

Hardware rounds to the nearest representable value, with ties to the one whose last bit is even (round-to-nearest-even). Its errors are unbiased on average but can systematically lose tiny updates (section 4). Stochastic rounding rounds up or down at random with probability proportional to distance; its expected value equals the true value. Some low-precision training methods and hardware support it.

Two things stand out. First, BF16 traded precision for range: it has FP32's exponent and only 7 mantissa bits, so it almost never overflows or underflows, but it is coarse. FP16 is the opposite: three times the precision but a maximum of 65,504 and a smallest normal of 6 × 10⁻⁵. Deep learning turned out to care much more about range, which is why BF16 won.

Second, FP4 can only represent 15 values: ±{0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6}. Everything about FP4 is in how you scale data into that tiny set.

4. How low precision breaks

There are four failure modes. Recognising them is most of the skill.

1. Overflow

Values above the maximum become infinity (or NaN in E4M3). Typical culprits: attention logits, exponentials in softmax, sums of squares in normalisation layers, and gradient norms. FP16 overflows at 65,504; E4M3 at 448. Fixes: compute these operations in FP32, subtract the max before exponentiating, scale before casting, and saturate (clamp to the format's maximum) rather than produce infinity.

2. Underflow

Values below the smallest subnormal become zero. Gradients are the classic victim: with FP16, many gradient values in deep networks fall below 6 × 10⁻⁸ and vanish. python mixed_precision_training.py shows it directly: with a small loss, 100% of FP16 gradients become exactly zero and the loss never moves, while BF16 and FP16-with-loss-scaling track FP32 exactly:

Mode Loss after epoch 1 After epoch 8 Gradients exactly zero
FP32 0.353 0.133 0%
BF16 autocast 0.353 0.133 0%
FP16, no scaling 0.922 0.922 100%
FP16 + GradScaler 0.353 0.133 0.2%

3. Swamping (lost additions)

Adding a small number to a large one in low precision can do nothing at all: if the small number is less than half a ULP of the large one, the sum rounds back to the large one. From python accumulation.py:

Summing 20,000 × 0.01 (true answer 200) Result
FP32 accumulator 199.97
FP16 accumulator 32.0
BF16 accumulator 4.0
BF16 + Kahan compensated summation 197.0
BF16 + stochastic rounding 201.0

The BF16 sum stops growing at 4.0 because BF16's spacing near 4 is 2⁻⁵ = 0.03125, and 0.01 is less than half of that. This is why every tensor-core matmul accumulates in FP32 even when its inputs are BF16 or FP8: a dot product of length 4,096 accumulated in BF16 had 13× the error of the same dot product accumulated in FP32 (6.7 × 10⁻² vs 5.0 × 10⁻³) in the same script.

The same effect hits weight updates. A BF16 weight of 1.0 cannot change by less than about 0.002: an update of 10⁻³ leaves it exactly at 1.0. With learning rates around 10⁻⁴ and small gradients, most updates would vanish. Hence FP32 master weights: the optimizer updates an FP32 copy, and BF16 is produced from it for the forward and backward passes.

4. Outliers

LLM activations are not well-behaved Gaussians. A few channels carry values tens to hundreds of times larger than the rest ("massive activations" or "outlier features"; Dettmers et al. found they emerge systematically in models above roughly 6.7B parameters). With integer formats, or any format with few values like FP4, a single scale per tensor must cover the outliers, leaving ordinary values crushed into a handful of levels. Section 6 shows the fix: finer-grained scales.

Key idea

Low-precision recipes follow one pattern: store and multiply in the narrow format, accumulate in FP32, keep numerically sensitive operations (softmax, normalisation statistics, loss, optimizer state, master weights) in higher precision, and scale tensors into the narrow format's range at a granularity fine enough to handle outliers.

5. Mixed-precision training

The standard recipe (Micikevicius et al., 2017), adapted for BF16:

In PyTorch, torch.autocast chooses the precision per operation: matmuls and convolutions run in BF16/FP16; reductions, softmax, normalisation, losses and pointwise ops that are sensitive to precision stay in FP32.

model = model.cuda()                       # parameters stay FP32 (the master copy)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)

# BF16: no loss scaling needed
for x, y in loader:
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
        loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
    loss.backward()
    opt.step(); opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)

# FP16: dynamic loss scaling
scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")
for x, y in loader:
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
        loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
    scaler.scale(loss).backward()          # multiply loss by S (e.g. 65,536) so gradients don't underflow
    scaler.step(opt)                       # unscale; skip the step if any gradient is inf/NaN
    scaler.update()                        # lower S after overflow, raise it after a run of good steps

Dynamic loss scaling multiplies the loss by a large factor S before backward, which shifts every gradient up by S (the chain rule is linear) and out of the underflow zone. Before the optimizer step, gradients are divided by S. If any gradient overflowed to infinity, the step is skipped and S is halved; after a run of good steps, S is doubled. BF16's range makes all of this unnecessary, which is a large part of why it replaced FP16.

Memory for training with this recipe is the 16 bytes per parameter from Chapter 5: 2 (BF16 weights) + 2 (BF16 gradients) + 4 (FP32 master) + 4 + 4 (Adam m and v). Reducing it further means storing optimizer state in lower precision (8-bit Adam, BF16 moments as in DeepSeek-V3) or sharding it (Deep Dive 17b).

Pitfall

TF32 is not enabled for matmuls by default in PyTorch, so a model written in FP32 runs its matmuls on slow paths unless you call torch.set_float32_matmul_precision("high"). Conversely, if you need exact FP32 results (for numerical tests), make sure TF32 is off. Many "my model is slow" and "my test fails on A100 but not on CPU" reports are this setting.

6. Scaling: fitting tensors into narrow formats

FP8 and FP4 cannot represent typical tensors directly: E4M3's maximum is 448 and FP4's is 6. Before casting, multiply by a scale so the tensor's largest magnitude (its amax) maps near the format's maximum; afterwards, the matmul result is multiplied by the inverse scales.

scale = amax(X) / format_max X_q = cast(X / scale) stored in FP8 / FP4 A·B ≈ (A_q · B_q) × scale_A × scale_B accumulated in FP32

The question is how many values share one scale.

python scaling.py quantizes an activation-like tensor (256 tokens × 4,096 channels with 8 large outlier channels) every way and reports the relative error:

Scheme Bits per value incl. scales Relative error
FP8 E4M3, per-tensor ~8 0.027
FP8 E4M3, per-row ~8 0.021
FP8 E4M3, 1×128 tiles (DeepSeek-V3) ~8.06 0.012
MXFP8 (blocks of 32, power-of-two scale) 8.25 0.026
FP4, per-tensor ~4 0.440
FP4, per-row ~4 0.374
MXFP4 (blocks of 32, power-of-two scale) 4.25 0.123
NVFP4 (blocks of 16, FP8 scale) 4.5 0.065

Two lessons:

  1. FP8 is fairly forgiving because it is still a floating-point format: each value keeps about the same relative precision wherever it sits in the range, so ordinary channels lose little even when an outlier sets a per-tensor scale. Finer tiles still halve the error.
  2. FP4 is not forgiving. With 15 values, a per-tensor scale destroys the tensor (44% error). Small blocks with their own scale are essential, and NVFP4's smaller blocks and more precise FP8 scales (instead of MXFP4's power-of-two scales) cut the error roughly in half again for a quarter of a bit more per value.

Delayed vs current scaling

Computing amax requires a pass over the tensor before the cast. Current (just-in-time) scaling computes it right away, which costs an extra read but is exact. Delayed scaling, used by early versions of NVIDIA's Transformer Engine, uses the maximum of amax values recorded over recent steps, so the scale is known before the tensor is produced and the cast can be fused into the producing kernel. It is cheaper but can be wrong when values jump suddenly, causing overflow and saturation. Fine-grained schemes compute the scale for each small block on the fly inside the kernel, which makes the problem disappear.

Microscaling (MX) and NVFP4

The OCP Microscaling (MX) specification (2023, backed by AMD, Arm, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, NVIDIA and Qualcomm) defines formats where each block of 32 elements shares one 8-bit power-of-two scale (E8M0): MXFP8, MXFP6, MXFP4 and MXINT8. NVFP4 is NVIDIA's variant: blocks of 16 FP4 values, each with an FP8 E4M3 scale, plus one FP32 scale per tensor. Blackwell tensor cores apply these block scales in hardware during the matmul, so fine-grained scaling costs almost nothing in speed.

7. FP8 and FP4 training in practice

The first FP8 recipes

Micikevicius et al. (2022) proposed E4M3 for weights and activations (more precision) and E5M2 for gradients (more range), with per-tensor delayed scaling. NVIDIA's Transformer Engine implemented this for Hopper and it was used for many FP8 training runs.

DeepSeek-V3's recipe (2024)

DeepSeek trained a 671B-parameter MoE model mostly in FP8 and published the details. The key choices:

  • E4M3 for all FP8 tensors, including gradients, made possible by fine-grained scaling: 1×128 tiles for activations (per token, per 128 channels) and 128×128 blocks for weights.
  • Higher-precision accumulation. They found that Hopper's FP8 tensor cores accumulate with limited precision (around 14 bits), which hurts long dot products. Their kernels periodically move partial sums into FP32 registers on the regular CUDA cores, every 128 elements along K. fp8_matmul.py simulates this pattern.
  • Keep sensitive parts in BF16/FP32: the embedding, the output head, MoE gating, normalisation and attention operations, as well as master weights, weight gradients and optimizer states (the Adam moments in BF16).
  • Result: they reported the loss of the FP8 model stayed within 0.25% of a BF16 baseline.

FP8 training is now common in frontier pretraining and supported in open frameworks: Transformer Engine, torchao.float8 (with FSDP2 integration), Megatron, and DeepSeek's open-source DeepGEMM kernels.

FP4 training

Training directly in FP4 is the 2025–26 frontier. Blackwell supports MXFP4 and NVFP4 matmuls in hardware, and NVIDIA has reported pretraining multi-billion-parameter models on trillions of tokens with NVFP4, using techniques such as stochastic rounding of gradients, random Hadamard transforms to spread outliers across a block, and keeping a small fraction of sensitive layers in higher precision. Treat FP4 training as research-grade: promising, hardware-specific, and still evolving.

8. Quantization for inference

Serving is where low precision is most widely used, because inference is memory-bound (15a) and has no gradients to protect. Deep Dive 19b covers the algorithms in depth; here are the numerical foundations.

Weight-only vs weight-and-activation

Scheme Weights Activations Speed-up comes from Common methods
W4A16 / W8A16 4 or 8 bits BF16/FP16 Fewer bytes per decode step (memory-bound) GPTQ, AWQ, bitsandbytes NF4
W8A8 (INT8) INT8 INT8 INT8 tensor cores + fewer bytes SmoothQuant
W8A8 (FP8) FP8 FP8 FP8 tensor cores; near-lossless for most models Per-channel / per-token scales; the default for large-model serving on Hopper+
W4A4 (NVFP4 / MXFP4) FP4 FP4 FP4 tensor cores on Blackwell Block scaling; often with calibration or quantization-aware training
KV cache FP8 / INT4 – KV cache More concurrent sequences and longer contexts Per-head or per-token scales

OpenAI's gpt-oss models (2025) shipped with their MoE weights natively in MXFP4, so the 120B model fits on a single 80 GB GPU.

Group-wise quantization

Integer weight quantization uses the same idea as block scaling: a scale (and sometimes a zero-point) per group of 64 or 128 weights along the input dimension. python weight_quantization.py compares schemes on a 2,048 × 4,096 weight matrix with rare large values, measuring the error of the layer's output for realistic inputs:

Method Bits per weight Output error Memory for a 70B model
INT8 per-tensor 8.0 0.067 70 GB
INT8 per-channel 8.0 0.027 70 GB
FP8 E4M3 per-channel 8.0 0.026 70 GB
INT4 per-channel 4.0 0.476 35 GB
INT4 group-128 symmetric 4.12 0.153 36 GB
INT4 group-128 asymmetric (zero-point) 4.25 0.114 37 GB
NF4 group-64 (QLoRA) 4.25 0.100 37 GB
FP4 blocks of 16, FP8 scale 4.5 0.103 39 GB

A fraction of a bit per weight spent on group scales cuts 4-bit error by 3–4×. NF4 (from QLoRA) uses 16 levels placed at the quantiles of a normal distribution instead of evenly spaced ones, matching how weights are actually distributed. Calibration-based methods (GPTQ, AWQ) reduce output error further by adjusting weights or scales using sample inputs.

PTQ vs QAT

Post-training quantization (PTQ) quantizes a finished model, sometimes with a small calibration set. Quantization-aware training (QAT) simulates quantization during training or fine-tuning (with a straight-through estimator for gradients) so the model learns to tolerate it. At 8 bits PTQ is usually enough; at 4 bits and below, QAT or careful calibration often makes the difference, and several labs now release QAT checkpoints for 4-bit deployment.

Pitfall

Average benchmark scores can hide quantization damage. Long-context retrieval, math, code and non-English text are often the first to degrade. Always evaluate quantized models on your own tasks and on long inputs before shipping (Deep Dive 23a).

10. Debugging numerics

NaN and infinity hunting

  1. Find the first bad step. Log loss, gradient norm and the max absolute value of activations per layer. NaNs usually appear after a spike.
  2. Find the first bad op. torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True) reports the backward op that produced a NaN (slow; use on one batch). Forward hooks that check torch.isfinite on each module's output locate forward problems.
  3. Usual suspects: division by a value that underflowed to zero, log(0), sqrt of a tiny negative from rounding, softmax without max-subtraction, FP16 overflow in attention logits, a learning rate that is too high, and corrupted data.
  4. Fixes: compute the operation in FP32, add an epsilon, clamp, use the numerically stable form (logsumexp, log_softmax), lower the learning rate, or add QK-norm or logit soft-capping for attention.

Loss spikes in large runs

Large runs occasionally spike even in BF16. Monitoring amax statistics of activations and gradients over time (which FP8 training needs anyway) gives early warning. Common mitigations: gradient clipping, QK-norm, lower learning rate for embeddings, skipping the offending batch and rolling back to a checkpoint (Chapter 10).

Determinism and batch invariance

Floating-point addition is not associative: (a + b) + c can differ from a + (b + c). GPU kernels split reductions differently depending on the number of threads, the order atomic operations complete in, and, importantly for serving, the batch size. So the same prompt can produce slightly different logits, and eventually different tokens, depending on what else was in the batch. Thinking Machines Lab's 2025 write-up Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference showed that making kernels batch-invariant (fixing the reduction order regardless of batch size) gives bitwise-reproducible outputs at a modest speed cost. This matters for RL training (where the sampler and trainer must agree on probabilities) and for debugging and evaluation.

torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)       # error on known non-deterministic ops
torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark = False
# plus: fixed seeds, fixed batch composition, and batch-invariant kernels for serving

Exercises

Decode the FP8 E4M3 bit pattern 0 1010 011. (Bias is 7.)

Show answer

Exponent 1010 = 10, so 2^(10 − 7) = 8. Mantissa 011 = 3/8, so the value is 1.375 × 8 = 11.0.

What is the spacing between adjacent BF16 numbers in [256, 512)? What happens if you add 1.0 to a BF16 value of 300?

Show answer

256 = 2⁸, and with 7 mantissa bits the spacing is 2^(8 − 7) = 2. Adding 1 to 300 gives 301, which is exactly halfway between 300 and 302; round-to-nearest-even keeps 300 (mantissa ending in 0). The addition is lost.

Why does FP16 training need loss scaling while BF16 training doesn't, even though FP16 has more precision?

Show answer

FP16's smallest subnormal is about 6 × 10⁻⁸, so small gradients underflow to zero. BF16 has FP32's 8-bit exponent and can represent values down to about 10⁻⁴⁰, so gradients practically never underflow. Range matters more than precision for gradients.

A tensor's amax is 1,200. What per-tensor scale maps it onto FP8 E4M3, and what is the largest error, relative to the value, for a value of 1,000 after quantization?

Show answer

scale = 1,200 / 448 ≈ 2.68. 1,000 / 2.68 ≈ 373.3, which lies in [256, 512) where E4M3 spacing is 32. It rounds to 384, which dequantizes to about 1,029: a 2.9% error. The worst case in that range is half the spacing, 16 / 256 ≈ 6.25% relative.

How many bits per value does NVFP4 cost, counting the block scale but ignoring the per-tensor scale? How much memory do the weights of a 405B model need in NVFP4?

Show answer

4 + 8/16 = 4.5 bits. 405 × 10⁹ × 4.5 / 8 ≈ 228 GB.

DeepSeek-V3 promotes FP8 partial sums to FP32 every 128 elements of K. Why not just accumulate everything in the tensor core?

Show answer

They measured that Hopper's FP8 tensor cores keep only about 14 bits of precision in the accumulator. For long inner dimensions (K in the thousands), the accumulated rounding error becomes significant (the swamping effect). Periodically adding partial results into true FP32 registers bounds the error, at a small cost because the transfer overlaps with other work.

Your RL training job uses vLLM to sample and PyTorch to compute log-probabilities, and the two disagree slightly even with identical weights. Give two possible numerical causes.

Show answer

Different kernels with different reduction orders (non-associative floating-point sums); batch-size-dependent reductions in the serving engine (lack of batch invariance); different precisions (e.g. FP8 KV cache or quantized weights in the sampler but BF16 in the trainer); different attention implementations or fused operations.

A W4A16 model serves faster than the BF16 model at batch size 1 but no faster at batch size 256. Explain why.

Show answer

At batch 1, decode is weight-bandwidth-bound, so reading 4× fewer bytes per step speeds it up. At batch 256, the matmuls approach compute-bound territory and still run in BF16 (weights are dequantized before the multiply), so fewer weight bytes no longer help, and dequantization adds work. W4A4 or FP8 W8A8, which use low-precision tensor cores, help at high batch sizes.

Build it

Project: Should we switch our fine-tuning to FP8?

Business problem: an ML team fine-tunes 8B models weekly on rented H100s. A vendor claims FP8 will cut their GPU bill by 30–40%. Leadership wants evidence before changing a production pipeline.

  1. Baseline. Fine-tune a 1B–8B model in BF16 with FSDP2 on one 8×H100 node (or a single H100 with a smaller model). Record tokens/s, peak memory, and the training and validation loss curves.
  2. FP8. Repeat with torchao.float8 (or Transformer Engine) using tensor-wise and then row-wise or block-wise scaling. Keep the embedding and output head in BF16.
  3. Compare quality. Overlay the loss curves, and evaluate both final models on the same task benchmark plus a long-context check. Quantify the difference.
  4. Find the edge cases. Log per-layer amax over training. Which layers have the largest dynamic range? Do any saturate?
  5. Recommend. Write a one-page decision memo: measured speed-up, cost saving per run at your GPU price, quality difference, and the risks you found. Include the cases where you would not use FP8.
